Anjan, **I would build a contemporary Indian textile house for exceptional interiors—not a broad handicrafts brand, and not a fashion label to begin with.**

The initial products would be **distinctive textile wall panels, exceptional cushions, and throws**, sold primarily through interior designers and architects, with a smaller private-client business.

Your advantage would be the ability to turn your knowledge and artisan relationships into **recognisable collections that can be produced reliably—not simply access to beautiful, rare things.**

And I would build the company from day one to be transferable to an acquirer: documented designs, repeat customers, dependable production, and a team that does not need you involved in every order.

Below is my recommendation, based on research available as of **23 September 2026**. I distinguish market evidence from the commercial assumptions we would need to test.

---

## 1. What the research says—and what it does not

### There is a growing Indian wealth base to sell into

Knight Frank’s 2026 research estimates that India’s population worth more than US$30 million grew approximately **63% between 2021 and 2026**, reaching nearly 20,000 people. Separately, CBRE’s July 2026 residential report says the share of high-end apartment sales continued to increase, despite a quarterly moderation in overall sales volumes. These are useful indicators for a luxury-interiors business. **They are not proof of demand for our particular products.** ([knightfrank.co.uk](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2026/4/twenty-years-of-the-wealth-report--lessons-from-two-decades-of-change))

### But “luxury is booming” would be the wrong investment thesis

Bain’s June 2026 update forecasts only a modest recovery in personal luxury goods and explicitly notes slowing design-and-furniture demand. It also describes weaker performance in Europe and the Middle East than in the Americas. I would therefore avoid a plan that assumes every luxury market will lift us automatically. ([prnewswire.com](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/global-luxury-stabilizes-amid-compounding-disruptions-as-brands-race-to-amplify-meaning-and-rebuild-relevance-302810874.html))

The risks are real even for established interiors businesses: on **21 September 2026**, Business of Home reported that Designers Guild had filed notices of intent to appoint an administrator while seeking investment. That is not a reason to reject the category; it is a reason to avoid expensive stores, excessive inventory, and growth without cash discipline. ([businessofhome.com](https://businessofhome.com/articles/designers-guild-takes-a-step-toward-bankruptcy))

### The category already exists—we would need a sharper proposition

Good Earth already offers craft-led cushions, including embroidered pieces listed around ₹8,000–₹12,500. Sarita Handa offers heritage-inspired embroidery, cushions, bedding, and furnishing fabrics. Internationally, de Gournay offers hand-painted fabrics and hand-embroidered wallcoverings. So “Indian craftsmanship for luxury homes” is **not an uncontested space**. ([goodearth.in](https://www.goodearth.in/collection/living_spring-cushions_586/?utm_source=openai))

**My inference:** the opportunity is not to introduce craftsmanship to wealthy customers. It is to create a *specific, desirable design identity* and make unusually good Indian textiles easy for professional designers to specify, purchase, and install.

## 2. Why I would choose interiors over the alternatives

This is my strategic ranking **for your background and your three-to-four-year objective**, not a claim that one category is universally better.

| Business | What attracts me | What makes me hesitate | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Designer-led textile interiors** | Strong use of your expertise; products can work across countries; repeat business can come through designers | Production consistency, sampling costs, and project sales cycles | **First choice** |
| Luxury scarves, shawls, and textile accessories | Compact products; gifting potential; direct connection to textile knowledge | We would still need to build consumer desirability and distribution | Strong second option |
| Luxury Indian occasionwear | Potential for exceptional craft and high-value purchases | Fit, alterations, seasonal collections, and dependence on a fashion identity | Not our starting point |
| Rare-textile collecting and bespoke commissions | Excellent fit with your knowledge | Risks becoming a founder-dependent advisory practice rather than a scalable brand | Small specialist line |
| Broad artisan marketplace | Allows representation of many crafts | Weak visual coherence; difficult quality control; little reason to choose our brand specifically | Avoid |

The deciding factor is this:

> **I would rather win 100 designers who repeatedly specify our collections than depend entirely on finding thousands of individual luxury shoppers.**

That is a distribution hypothesis we can test relatively quickly.

## 3. Exactly what we should build

### The proposition

**A contemporary textile design house, rooted in Indian craft, creating distinctive objects for exceptional homes.**

Not a catalogue organised around every region you have visited. Not a collection held together only by the word “handmade.”

We need a visual language strong enough that a customer can recognise our work before reading its provenance.

### Launch with one collection and approximately 12 designs

| Product | Initial range | Proposed retail price to test* | Commercial role |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **Signature cushion covers** | 6 designs | ₹15,000–₹30,000 each | Repeatable core; multiple pieces per room |
| **Throws / decorative coverlets** | 3 designs | ₹40,000–₹90,000 | Larger purchase; gifting and private clients |
| **Textile wall panels** | 3 designs | ₹1–₹4 lakh each | Distinctive signature product; higher-value projects |
| Bespoke installations | By exception | Quoted individually | Select prestige projects—not the operating model |

\*These are **pricing hypotheses, excluding taxes**, not validated willingness-to-pay or supplier quotations. Export pricing would need a separate landed-cost calculation.

These prices put us above many established domestic offerings. We must earn that premium through the object itself: design, materials, execution, finishing, presentation, and service. A compelling artisan story alone will not be our justification.

### The product architecture matters

I would divide the business into:

- **Core collection:** repeatable designs, controlled variations, replenishable production.
- **Numbered editions:** genuinely limited works using more demanding techniques.
- **Bespoke:** a tightly capped service for exceptional commissions.

My proposed eventual revenue mix is approximately **70% repeatable collection, 20% editions, 10% bespoke**.

That preserves room for extraordinary craftsmanship without turning every sale into a new development project.

**I would not initially launch rugs, furniture, full bedding ranges, apparel, and tableware.** Nor would I start with technical upholstery or full commercial wallcovering systems before the relevant testing and installation capabilities are in place.

## 4. Where your expertise becomes a defensible advantage

Your network is the starting asset. The business needs to turn it into five repeatable capabilities.

### A. A selective craft portfolio

Start with **two or three techniques**, selected jointly for:

- Visual distinctiveness.
- Suitability for the intended product.
- Repeatability and quality.
- Available artisan capacity.
- Sustainable production economics.

I would not choose a craft solely because it is endangered. We should support fragile traditions, but we should not place commercial volume commitments on a production base that cannot responsibly fulfil them.

### B. Original co-developed designs

Commission new compositions rather than simply placing existing traditional motifs on expensive objects.

Document design development and agree clearly on attribution, compensation, usage, and any exclusivity. We should distinguish rights in our new work from ownership of a community’s traditional expressions. WIPO specifically highlights the importance of respectful engagement, consent, and equitable collaboration when working with such cultural material. ([wipo.int](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/traditional-knowledge/fashion?utm_source=openai))

### C. A production system

For every repeatable design, build:

- An approved reference sample.
- Material and colour specifications.
- Acceptable handmade variation.
- Cost and production-time records.
- Quality checks and repair instructions.
- Capacity plans agreed with the artisans.

### D. A designer-friendly service

The designer should receive physical samples, accurate dimensions, useful photographs, clear quotations, dependable delivery updates, and responsive problem resolution.

### E. A company-owned institutional memory

Your knowledge needs to become a documented design and production library—with appropriate permissions—not remain entirely in your head.

**We should make you essential to the company’s creation, but progressively less essential to its daily fulfilment.**

## 5. Who we should sell to

I would replace “the wealthiest 1%” with a more actionable customer definition:

> **A design-conscious household furnishing or refreshing an expensive home, working with a professional designer, and willing to allocate ₹3–₹10 lakh to distinctive textile elements in a project.**

That is our proposed initial customer profile—not a measured market statistic.

### The first customers I would pursue

1. **Residential interior-design studios** serving wealthy clients.
2. **Private clients** introduced through those studios, your relationships, and small presentations.
3. **Art and design advisers** interested in textile wall pieces.
4. **Selected boutique-hospitality projects**, once we have proven delivery.

I would not make hotel room linen, large procurement tenders, or anonymous private-label manufacturing our initial focus.

### Geographic sequence

**First: India.**  
Start in whichever of Mumbai or Delhi NCR gives us the strongest warm introductions. Add Bengaluru through specific relationships rather than opening another base.

**Second: one US trade-sales pilot.**  
Test a small group of design studios through a representative or partner showroom. Do not begin with a national consumer-advertising campaign.

**UK and UAE: relationship-led pilots, not simultaneous full launches.**  
We can interview buyers in all three export markets now, but commit inventory and sales expenditure only where we obtain paid projects at acceptable margins.

Given Bain’s uneven regional outlook, I would not automatically put Dubai first simply because it is associated with luxury. ([prnewswire.com](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/global-luxury-stabilizes-amid-compounding-disruptions-as-brands-race-to-amplify-meaning-and-rebuild-relevance-302810874.html))

For exports, price each SKU after checking classification, freight, duties, applicable taxes, and local requirements. US textile labelling rules cover many home-textile products, with product-specific exceptions; this needs SKU-level review rather than one generic “export-ready” label. ([ftc.gov](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/threading-your-way-through-labeling-requirements-under-textile-wool-acts?utm_source=openai))

## 6. How the business could make money

### An illustrative cushion transaction

This is a **domestic planning example**, not a forecast:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Retail price, excluding tax | ₹24,000 |
| Designer discount: 30% | −₹7,200 |
| **Our net revenue** | **₹16,800** |
| Product cost, including materials, artisan payment, finishing, and QC | −₹6,000 |
| **Gross profit** | **₹10,800 / 64%** |
| Sales commission | −₹1,680 |
| Fulfilment and claims allowance | −₹1,500 |
| Allocated sampling expense | −₹800 |
| **Contribution before fixed overhead** | **₹6,820 / 41%** |

That contribution still has to pay salaries, design development, rent, travel, administration, and other overhead.

For export orders, we must rebuild the calculation rather than assume these margins survive.

My proposed commercial rules:

- Price from **net realised revenue**, not the headline retail price.
- Obtain deposits for made-to-order work.
- Negotiate artisan advances into our working-capital plan.
- Carry limited finished stock.
- Avoid open-ended custom revisions.
- Track profitability by product, customer, and destination.

If the economics only work by underpaying artisans or ignoring rework and selling costs, the product does not work.

### What meaningful scale could look like

An illustrative annual revenue build:

- **150 active design studios × 4 projects × ₹3 lakh net revenue:** ₹18 crore.
- **250 direct private-client orders × ₹2 lakh:** ₹5 crore.
- **10 separate hospitality or special projects × ₹20 lakh:** ₹2 crore.

**Total: ₹25 crore**, with no double-counting between channels.

This is an ambitious operating scenario, not a demand forecast. It shows the sales organisation we would need to build. At a hypothetical 18% EBITDA margin, it would produce ₹4.5 crore of EBITDA—but neither that margin nor any exit valuation should be assumed today.

## 7. How I would approach the exit

**A sale in three to four years is a goal, not something we can responsibly promise.**

There are relevant precedents:

- F. Schumacher acquired **Backdrop in May 2021**, after Backdrop’s founding in 2018. However, Backdrop was a paint brand, so this is evidence that young adjacent design brands can attract buyers—not a direct valuation comparable. ([prnewswire.com](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/f-schumacher--co-acquires-backdrop-301289745.html))
- Schumacher acquired **Tillett Textiles in 2025**, demonstrating interest in a distinctive textile identity and archive. Tillett had decades of heritage, and the acquisition price was not disclosed. ([businessofhome.com](https://businessofhome.com/articles/schumacher-acquires-tillett-textiles))

The broader design-library model also has commercial precedent: Sanderson Design Group reported **£10.5 million in licensing revenue for the year ended 31 January 2026**. That supports the potential value of designs and brands beyond manufacturing, but it does not mean a new archive will immediately generate licensing demand. ([sandersondesign.group](https://sandersondesign.group/media/2165/annual-report-2026.pdf))

### The buyer profiles I would investigate

- International textile and interiors groups seeking a differentiated collection.
- Premium home businesses seeking stronger Indian sourcing and design capabilities.
- Indian home-textile businesses seeking a branded, higher-value customer channel.

These are potential buyer categories—not indications of actual interest.

### What I would want ready by 2029–2030

- A recognisable, company-owned brand.
- Clearly documented rights and artisan agreements.
- Repeat orders from professional customers.
- Reliable margins and clean accounts.
- Limited dependence on any one customer.
- A product-development and operations team.
- Evidence that customers buy the brand, not only a personal relationship with Anjan.

I would begin strategic relationships during year two, but build a business we can profitably keep if no attractive offer arrives.

## 8. The first 90 days: prove demand before building infrastructure

I would authorise a **₹15–₹25 lakh validation budget**, subject to actual quotations—not a showroom lease or a large inventory purchase.

### Days 1–30: buyer discovery

Interview:

- 15 Indian luxury residential designers.
- 10 designers across the US, UK, and UAE.
- 10 relevant private buyers.

Ask about recent purchases, actual budgets, supplier disappointments, acceptable lead times, and how products get approved.

Do not ask only, “Do you like this idea?”

### Days 31–60: develop and cost prototypes

- Develop six to eight strong prototypes.
- Work with two or three production partners.
- Prepare physical samples, product specifications, and room-setting photography.
- Establish full costs and realistic lead times.
- Present real prices.

Your first complementary hire should be a **commercially experienced textile/interiors design lead**, unless that capability already exists in your team. Deep textile knowledge and the ability to create a coherent commercial collection are related, but not identical.

### Days 61–90: sell

Seek:

- At least five paid pilot projects.
- Orders from at least three unrelated customers.
- At least two paid export trials.
- Positive contribution after fulfilment and selling costs.
- Evidence of demand for repeatable designs—not only bespoke requests.

**My stop-or-change signal:** enthusiastic praise, requests for free samples, and press interest—but no deposits at prices that support the business.

## My recommendation

**Build a tightly edited, designer-led textile interiors house, with textile wall panels as the signature and repeatable cushions and throws as the commercial foundation.**

Use your breadth of knowledge to make better choices—not to launch more categories.

The central bet is:

> **Can we turn exceptional Indian textile knowledge into products that designers repeatedly choose, at prices that fairly fund craftsmanship and leave a profitable, transferable business?**

That is the question I would spend the first 90 days answering.

Before committing beyond that, I would want four answers from you:

1. **How much capital can you comfortably commit before the business supports itself?**
2. **Do you already have relationships with wealthy buyers, architects, or interior designers—or primarily with artisans?**
3. **Which three craft relationships could support consistent commercial production today?**
4. **What would a successful exit mean in personal proceeds, and would you be willing to stay involved after a sale?**



## Competitor businesses globally

Anjan, **the closest competitors to the business I proposed are A Rum Fellow, Seema Krish, Lesage Intérieurs/Vastrakala, Fromental, de Gournay, and Sarita Handa.**

They are more useful comparisons than simply looking at famous luxury brands because they overlap with the actual proposition: **craft-led textiles, distinctive wall pieces, luxury soft furnishings, and sales through interior designers.**

After looking more closely at their current offerings, I would sharpen our earlier thesis: **“Indian craftsmanship for luxury interiors” is already well served. We would need a much more specific design identity and reason to buy.**

Here is the competitive map, based primarily on the brands’ own websites, checked in **September 2026**. The assessments of relevance are my analysis—not rankings by revenue or market share.

## 1. The six closest competitors

### 1. A Rum Fellow — United Kingdom
**Closest comparison for the overall artisan-led business model**

A Rum Fellow creates handwoven fabrics, brocade panels, cushions, woven artworks, and rugs. It works with artisan partners in Guatemala, India, and Nepal, translating traditional techniques into contemporary collections. Its brocade collections explicitly bridge functional textiles and artworks. ([arumfellow.com](https://www.arumfellow.com/wild-abandon-collection-story))

**Why it matters to us:** This is remarkably close to the proposed combination of artisan relationships, contemporary design, textile art, and repeatable furnishings.

**What I would study:**
- How a consistent visual identity connects different techniques.
- How it presents artisans without making the product feel like a charity purchase.
- How one design language extends across fabrics, cushions, and artwork.

It also has a wallpaper collaboration with **Schumacher**—a useful example of a specialist craft-led studio working with an established design business. That is a collaboration, not evidence of an acquisition. ([arumfellow.com](https://www.arumfellow.com/arf-x-schumacher))

---

### 2. Seema Krish — United States, with production in India
**Closest comparison for bringing Indian textile craft into the US designer market**

Seema Krish develops textiles and wallcoverings, with design based in San Francisco and handcrafted textile production in Bangalore. Its range uses techniques including hand-block printing, embroidery, weaving, and shibori. It also publishes a structured ordering process covering samples, stock reservations, custom orders, payments, and claims. ([seemakrish.com](https://seemakrish.com/?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters to us:** It is a direct challenge to any claim that we would be uniquely connecting Indian craftsmanship with contemporary American interiors.

**What I would study:**
- The aesthetic translation from Indian technique to an international interior.
- Product specifications and sample presentation.
- Designer ordering terms and showroom relationships.

**My assessment:** One of the most useful operational benchmarks for our export model—not just a brand to admire visually.

---

### 3. Lesage Intérieurs / Vastrakala — France and India
**Closest comparison for exceptional bespoke embroidery and architectural textile commissions**

Lesage Intérieurs develops hand-embroidered interiors projects, including wall panels, screens, and historical decorative work. Its production relationship with **Vastrakala in Chennai** combines French and Indian expertise, serving architects, decorators, museums, designers, and collectors. ([lesageinterieurs.com](https://lesageinterieurs.com/))

**Why it matters to us:** It demonstrates that Indian embroidery is already being positioned at the highest end of international decorative arts.

**What I would study:**
- The quality and scale of its commissions.
- How designs are developed for an architectural setting.
- How craftsmanship is presented as artistic expertise rather than simply labour.

**My assessment:** A particularly important benchmark for our proposed statement wall pieces. Less directly comparable to the repeatable cushion-and-throw side of the business.

---

### 4. Fromental — United Kingdom
**Closest comparison for the proposed product range**

Fromental offers bespoke handcrafted wallcoverings alongside ready-to-order artworks, cushions, throws, and wall hangings. Its accessories use techniques including hand painting, embroidery, and needlepoint. ([fromental.com](https://fromental.com/collections/all-accessories))

**Why it matters to us:** Its product architecture resembles what I suggested: prestigious wall treatments and artwork, supported by smaller finished products.

Two current examples illustrate its positioning:
- **Unconscious Peony cushion:** **£450 including VAT**, supplied with filling.
- **Rockface–El Capitan wall hanging:** **£10,500 including VAT**. ([fromental.com](https://fromental.com/products/unconscious-peony-famille-rose-cushion-composition-a))

**What I would study:**
- The separation between bespoke and ready-to-order products.
- How collections move between walls and accessories.
- Designer collaborations and product photography.

---

### 5. de Gournay — United Kingdom
**A benchmark for the upper end of decorative textile luxury**

Beyond hand-painted wallpaper, de Gournay offers hand-painted fabrics and fully hand-embroidered wallcoverings. Its embroidery can be applied to silk, linen, velvet, wool sateen, and client-supplied fabrics, using materials such as silk thread, metallic thread, beads, and raffia. ([degournay.com](https://degournay.com/fabric?utm_source=openai))

**Why it matters to us:** It competes directly for the kind of distinctive, high-value wall treatment a wealthy client might consider instead of our textile panels.

**What I would study:**
- The visual impact of the finished room.
- Customisation choices.
- How the brand presents craftsmanship and materials.

**My assessment:** A quality and positioning benchmark—not a sensible company to imitate in breadth at launch.

---

### 6. Sarita Handa — India
**A more direct domestic competitor than my earlier overview suggested**

Sarita Handa’s range includes embroidered cushions, furnishing fabrics, bedding, and **embroidered fabric wallcoverings**. Its wallcovering collection includes abstract, floral, and tonal designs, while its website also promotes a professional partner programme. ([saritahanda.com](https://saritahanda.com/))

**Why it matters to us:** We cannot treat embroidered wall panels or Indian craft-led interiors as an unoccupied domestic category.

**What I would study:**
- Where our proposed collection would look genuinely different beside theirs.
- Price, finishing, customisation, and delivery comparisons.
- Why a designer would add us to an existing supplier list.

**My assessment:** An essential competitor to evaluate in person before finalising our first collection.

For our specific decision, I would prioritise:

## What this means for our positioning

My conclusion is **not** that the category is too crowded. It is that our differentiation must be narrower than:

> “Rare Indian crafts, beautiful stories, and luxury prices.”

The territory I would test is:

> **Original contemporary textile compositions, co-developed with specific Indian artisan traditions, presented as a tightly edited collection of wall works and coordinated furnishings—with unusually clear provenance and dependable designer service.**

That is still a hypothesis, not a proven gap.

**The deciding question is: when our sample sits beside A Rum Fellow, Fromental, Seema Krish, or Sarita Handa, is there an obvious reason to choose ours—even before you explain your background?**
