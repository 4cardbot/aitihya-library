# Aitihya: business blueprint

**Research cut-off:** 26 September 2026  
**Purpose:** operating blueprint for a 36–48 month build, scale and potential strategic sale.  
**Inputs:** `business_plans/1.md` through `business_plans/7.md`, the previous strategic recommendation, and current first-party market, competitor, technology and standards sources.

## 1. Executive decision

Build **Aitihya** as a **research-led Indian textile design house for exceptional interiors**.

Use **Pramāṇa** as the evidence, provenance and digital product-certification layer behind the products.

The company will turn Anjan’s field knowledge and artisan relationships into a recognisable contemporary product system:

- repeatable cushions, throws and coverlets as the commercial engine;
- numbered textile wall panels and larger textile objects as the halo;
- tightly controlled bespoke commissions for exceptional residences and designers;
- a designer/architect trade channel supported by private-client appointments.

Do not launch as a broad handicrafts marketplace, bridal/apparel label, hotel-linen supplier, pure art gallery or technology company.

The strategic advantage is not simply “access to artisans.” It is the combination of **selection, design authorship, production reliability, provenance and a high-touch specification service**.

## 2. Synthesis of the seven consultant plans

|---|---|---|

| Plan 6 | Rare fibres, quiet-luxury aesthetic, material-first positioning | Adopt material focus; avoid promising “Loro Piana of South Asia” before proof |
| Plan 7 | Twelve-design launch, realistic buyer discovery, paid-pilot gates, contribution economics | Adopt as the launch operating discipline |

### The final strategic synthesis

Keep:

- home and architectural textiles over fitted apparel;
- a halo-and-engine product architecture;
- rare techniques and fibres as authority, not as the entire volume base;
- trade-first distribution;
- private salons instead of early flagships;
- a documented, transferable production system;
- paid demand validation before infrastructure.

Reject or defer:

- a simultaneous home, apparel, bedding, rugs, furniture and hospitality launch;
- claims of 75–88% gross margin before full costing;
- “zero returns” and “zero CAC” assumptions;
- GPS tracking of products or artisans;
- blockchain/NFT infrastructure without a customer problem;
- a guaranteed $50–100M exit;
- treating LVMH, Kering or any named buyer as committed demand;
- exclusivity over whole artisan communities or traditional cultural expressions.

## 3. Brand architecture, positioning and promise

### 3.1 The names

The naming relationship is strong:

- **Aitihya** is the customer-facing product house: textiles made for hosting, dwelling, gifting and living well.
- **Pramāṇa** is the evidence layer: the system that records, verifies and communicates what a product is, where it came from, how it was made and what claims can be supported.

The names create a useful relationship between **experience and proof**:

> Aitihya is what the client lives with. Pramāṇa is why the client can trust it.

Use **Aitihya** consistently in the premium visual identity, certificates and editorial material. Maintain a practical uppercase fallback—`AITIHYA` and `PRAMANA`—for domains, email, search, shipping systems, customs and software fields. Before public launch, run Indian and international trademark, domain and social-handle clearance. Also test pronunciation with Indian, Middle Eastern, UK and US buyers; the brand should be teachable in one sentence.

### 3.2 What Pramāṇa is—and is not

At launch, Pramāṇa should be described as **Aitihya’s provenance and product-certification registry**, not as an independent third-party certifier.

If Pramāṇa is owned and operated by the same entity that designs and sells Aitihya products, it can verify records against internal standards, but it must not imply impartial external certification. That would create avoidable trust and regulatory risk.

Use a staged model:

1. **Stage 1 — Pramāṇa Registry:** internal chain-of-custody, product IDs, maker permissions, material records, QC and digital certificates.
2. **Stage 2 — Pramāṇa Standard:** published criteria, version-controlled protocols, defined evidence thresholds and a review panel including external textile, conservation, technical and community representatives.
3. **Stage 3 — Independent Pramāṇa Foundation or certification body:** only if the volume, governance and commercial case justify a legally and operationally separate entity.

Until Stage 3 exists, use language such as **“Pramāṇa-registered,” “Pramāṇa-documented” or “Pramāṇa-verified against the Pramāṇa Standard.”** Reserve **“independently certified”** for products reviewed by an actually independent body.

### 3.3 Pramāṇa certificate classes

Create clearly named certificate classes so customers understand what has been checked:

| Class | What it verifies | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| P1 — Origin record | Region/cluster, workshop, authorised attribution and production period | Core cushions, throws and coverlets |
| P2 — Material record | Fibre composition, yarn, dye/process information and relevant test evidence | Rare-fibre and material-led products |
| P3 — Edition record | Unique ID, edition number, maker, design version, dimensions, condition and ownership history | Numbered wall panels and collector pieces |
| P4 — Technical record | Acoustic, fire, lightfastness, abrasion, fixing or installation evidence for the complete system | Architectural and acoustic products |
| P5 — Conservation record | Condition, repair, cleaning, restoration and post-sale service history | Older, repaired or high-value pieces |

Every certificate should state its scope and limitations. A P1 origin record must not be read as a guarantee of fibre purity; a P4 acoustic record must identify the tested assembly, installation and test standard.

### 3.4 Working proposition

> **Contemporary textiles for exceptional rooms, made through India’s living textile knowledge.**

The brand should feel quiet, precise and intellectually confident. It should not look like a tourist craft store, an NGO catalogue, a fashion label or a museum gift shop.

### 3.5 What the client should understand immediately

1. The object is visually distinctive.
2. The material and construction are exceptional.
3. The object has a specific place, process and maker history.
4. It works in a contemporary home.
5. The brand can deliver it professionally.

Anjan’s anthropology is the source of the authority. It should appear as evidence, editorial depth and provenance—not as academic jargon on every product page.

### 3.6 Brand pillars

1. **Material intelligence:** fibre, yarn, dye, structure and hand-feel.
2. **Living knowledge:** the people and communities who hold the technique.
3. **Contemporary authorship:** original compositions and controlled adaptations.
4. **Domestic permanence:** objects made to live with, repair and pass on.
5. **Professional reliability:** accurate samples, specifications, timelines and installation.

## 4. Product architecture

### 4.1 Launch portfolio: 70/20/10

| Layer | Initial quantity | Product formats | Commercial role |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Core engine | 6–8 designs | Cushions, coverlets, throws, small table textiles | Repeat orders, room completion, accessible entry to the house |
| Numbered editions | 3–4 designs | Textile wall panels, framed works, large textile objects | Scarcity, press, collector demand, higher AOV |
| Bespoke | 1–3 pilots | Custom panels, room groupings, limited installations | Learning and prestige; capped so it does not consume the company |

Launch with approximately 12 designs, but limit the number of fibres, dyes, finishing methods and production partners. Variety should come from composition and scale, not operational sprawl.

### 4.2 Craft selection matrix

Select one structural craft, one surface/intervention craft and one rare-fibre story only after scoring the actual relationships available to Anjan.

Score each candidate from 1–5 on:

| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Visual signature | Can a designer recognise it without reading the story? |
| Contemporary translation | Does it work in a quiet modern room without becoming folkloric? |
| Capacity | Can the cluster produce agreed quantities at a known lead time? |
| Repeatability | Can approved designs be reproduced within a defined tolerance? |
| Material stability | Will colour, tension, shrinkage and hand-feel remain acceptable? |
| Economics | Can it support artisan pay, trade margins, finishing and profit? |
| Product fit | Is it suitable for a cushion, throw, panel or commission? |
| Export readiness | Can it be packed, labelled, shipped and cared for safely? |
| Cultural protocol | Who can consent, approve attribution and receive benefits? |
| Founder independence | Can another trained team member manage the relationship later? |

Possible research candidates may include rare ikat or brocade structures, Kani or other complex woven traditions, surface techniques such as Rogan or Chamba Rumal, and rare fibres such as Muga, Eri or high-grade pashm. These are examples for the shortlist, not automatic launch choices.

### 4.3 Product rules

- Every repeatable design receives a master sample, tolerance sheet, cost card and lead-time promise.
- Every numbered work receives a condition report, edition number, maker attribution, material record and signed authenticity document.
- Bespoke work requires a written brief, approval sample, deposit, change-order rule and final sign-off.
- No product is made “custom” merely because the internal process is unclear.
- No endangered technique is used as a volume promise until capacity and community consent are confirmed.

### 4.4 Indicative price tests

These are domestic planning hypotheses, excluding tax and subject to customer testing:

- cushions or paired small textiles: ₹15,000–₹40,000;
- throws and coverlets: ₹50,000–₹1.5 lakh;
- numbered wall panels: ₹2–₹6 lakh;
- bespoke commissions: ₹8 lakh upward.

Export prices should be rebuilt from landed cost, duties/taxes, distributor or showroom margin, insurance, installation and after-sales—not converted using an exchange rate alone.

## 5. Provenance, GPS and product technology

### 5.1 The correct interpretation of “GPS tag”

Do not put a live GPS tracker inside a textile. A textile tag cannot reliably track location, and a live tracker would create battery, privacy, cost and disposal problems.

Use **geographic provenance**, not live location tracking:

- store the village, district or landscape of origin;
- record the atelier or workshop with consent;
- use exact coordinates internally where necessary for research and supply management;
- display an approximate map point or broader region publicly unless the community has expressly approved greater precision;
- never expose a maker’s home address, phone number or sensitive cultural site on a public page.

The public language should say “woven in the [region] cluster” or “made by the [atelier/community]” unless a more precise statement is authorised and meaningful.

### 5.2 Product identity system

Every product gets a permanent **Aitihya Product ID** linked to a secure Pramāṇa record.

Recommended fields:

- product ID and edition number;
- design name and version;
- product category and dimensions;
- fibre composition and material batch;
- technique and process description;
- region, atelier and maker attribution;
- production dates and quality-control status;
- approved variation range;
- care instructions and repair pathway;
- test reports where relevant;
- ownership/transfer history only with client consent;
- claims and evidence, separated from marketing language.

### 5.3 Tag hierarchy

| Product | Physical identifier | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Core cushions/throws | Printed QR on care/provenance label | Low-cost access to care, origin and product record |
| High-value core products | QR plus passive NFC | Easier tap experience and analytics |
| Numbered panels and commissions | Secure NFC plus QR fallback, mounted on reverse or certificate | Authentication, transfer, conservation and edition record |
| Shipping cartons and sample boxes | Internal barcode/QR or RFID | Inventory and fulfilment; not a consumer-facing luxury feature |

For high-value editions, a secure NFC technology such as a cryptographically authenticated tag can help distinguish an original tag from a copied URL. NXP’s NTAG 424 DNA, for example, supports AES-128 authentication, protected data and tamper-aware variants. This is an implementation option, not a requirement to use that exact chip. [NXP NTAG 424 DNA](https://www.nxp.com/products/rfid-nfc/nfc-hf/ntag-for-tags-and-labels/ntag-424-dna-424-dna-tagtamper-advanced-security-and-privacy-for-trusted-iot-applications%3ANTAG424DNA)

### 5.4 Digital product passport architecture

Build a simple first-party digital passport now so the data exists even if a destination market later requires a formal standard.

The passport should have three layers:

1. **Public:** object story, materials, region, technique, care, repair and authenticity status.
2. **Trade:** dimensions, lead time, test reports, installation, sample references and approved use.
3. **Internal:** cost, supplier, exact coordinates, agreements, QC images, payment and capacity information.

The EU Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation requires the digital product passport framework to use a data carrier linked to a persistent unique product identifier, with accurate, complete and up-to-date data. Textile-specific requirements and delegated acts must still be monitored. Build interoperable data, not a closed NFT system. [EUR-Lex ESPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1781/eng)

### 5.5 Digital experience

The product scan can offer:

- a short film of the process;
- a map at the approved level of precision;
- a maker or atelier profile;
- material and care information;
- a “how variation is part of the hand” explanation;
- repair or conservation booking;
- transfer or gifting registration;
- a room image or installation guidance;
- a private designer download area.

Do not overload the tag with a long film, speculative carbon claims or unverifiable “last master” language. The scan should be useful in under 30 seconds, with deeper research available below it.

### 5.6 The cinematic product documentary

Every product should have a **Pramāṇa Story Page** reached through its QR code. For high-value products, the page should open with a short, cinematic documentary about that specific object—not a generic brand film.

The film should show:

- the material before it became the product;
- the relevant landscape or production region at an approved level of precision;
- the technique in use;
- the artisan, atelier or finishing team where consent has been given;
- the moment of finishing, inspection and certification;
- the final object in a contemporary room;
- what variation, care and future repair mean for this piece.

The QR should resolve to a **dynamic URL**, not a permanently printed video URL. This allows Aitihya to update subtitles, care instructions, ownership status or a repaired-product record without replacing the physical label.

#### Three documentary levels

Do not produce a completely bespoke film for every cushion. Use a modular documentary system:

| Product level | Film | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Core product | 45–75 second collection film with product-specific overlay | Material, technique, region, care and the collection story |
| Numbered edition | 2–4 minute object documentary | Exact edition, production journey, maker/atelier, finishing and condition |
| Bespoke commission | 5–8 minute private film | Commission brief, collaboration, making, installation and client-approved room story |

The product ID, edition number and Pramāṇa record should be rendered dynamically in the film page. This creates personal relevance without pretending that every unit required a separate film shoot.

#### Documentary standards

- Shoot in both a 16:9 master and vertical/social cut.
- Include English subtitles and an accurate transcript from the first release.
- Add Hindi and other market-language subtitles where the story or market justifies it.
- Provide an audio-description or text-only alternative for accessibility.
- Never reveal exact maker addresses, unsafe locations or sensitive cultural sites without permission.
- Obtain written consent for faces, voices, names, places, music, archive images and stories.
- Give makers and communities the right to review factual descriptions and to withdraw sensitive material before publication.
- Store a high-resolution master, an access copy, captions, transcript, consent records, music licence and usage expiry in the Pramāṇa media archive.

#### Personalisation options

For a registered owner, the story page can add:

- the owner’s name or gifting message, only behind a private access layer;
- purchase date and edition number;
- room or installation photographs uploaded with permission;
- repair and conservation history;
- a transfer-of-ownership function;
- a private note from Anjan or the atelier for a commission.

Do not expose a client’s name, home, purchase value or room photographs on a public QR page by default.

## 6. Digital and software business architecture

Digital should perform four jobs:

1. Make the brand desirable before the client touches the product.
2. Make Aitihya easy for designers to specify and order.
3. Make Pramāṇa evidence trustworthy and maintainable.
4. Make the company more transferable and valuable to a future acquirer.

Do not build a large custom app in Year 1. Use reliable modular software with clear ownership of data, then build custom components only where provenance, production knowledge or trade workflow creates a defensible advantage.

### 6.1 The digital ecosystem

| System | Job | First version |
|---|---|---|
| Brand website | Editorial, collections, product discovery and enquiries | Public website with appointment booking and limited checkout |
| Pramāṇa Registry | Product ID, provenance, certificates, QR/NFC status and audit trail | Secure database with role-based admin and public Story Pages |
| Product information system | Dimensions, materials, care, pricing, lead times and specifications | Structured product records reused across web, trade and sales documents |
| Digital asset management | Master films, images, captions, transcripts, certificates and usage rights | Searchable archive with version and consent metadata |
| Trade portal | Designer samples, downloadable specifications, pricing and project enquiries | Password-protected portal or controlled client workspace |
| CRM | Designers, private clients, galleries, hospitality and referral relationships | Account-based pipeline with next action and sample status |
| Order/inventory/production system | Quotes, deposits, production stages, stock, QC and dispatch | Shared operational system linked to the Pramāṇa Product ID |
| Finance system | Invoices, payments, landed cost, margin and cash reporting | Product/channel/customer-level reporting |
| Analytics and consent | Website, QR, film and funnel measurement | Privacy-conscious, aggregated analytics with opt-outs |
| Customer-care portal | Care, repairs, re-authentication and ownership transfer | Start with a service request form linked to Product ID |

### 6.2 The single source of truth

The same product identity must flow through every stage:

```text
Workshop record
    ↓
Pramāṇa Product ID
    ↓
Prototype / QC / certificate
    ↓
Website / trade portal / QR / NFC
    ↓
Quote / order / invoice / dispatch
    ↓
Owner care / repair / transfer history
```

Do not create separate product names or IDs in the website, finance system, workshop spreadsheets and certificates. That is how provenance systems become unreliable.

### 6.3 Public website structure

The site should feel like a private design house with a useful archive, not a crowded catalogue.

Recommended navigation:

- **Collections:** core, numbered editions and commissions.
- **Materials:** fibre, yarn, dye and hand-feel library.
- **Techniques:** carefully edited research and process stories.
- **Rooms:** installed work, designer projects and case studies.
- **Pramāṇa:** what is recorded, how verification works and what each certificate means.
- **Journal:** field notes, interviews, films and conservation stories.
- **Trade:** request a sample library, download specifications and book a presentation.
- **Visit/Enquire:** appointments, private salons and commission briefs.

Every product page should show:

- clear dimensions and materials;
- price or “enquire” status;
- lead time and made-to-order status;
- care requirements;
- trade suitability;
- a short film;
- Pramāṇa certificate class;
- sample request and appointment CTA.

### 6.4 Trade portal

The trade portal is more valuable than a sophisticated consumer app in the first two years.

It should let a designer:

- view high-resolution material and room images;
- request or track sample loans;
- download spec sheets, dimensions and installation drawings;
- see trade pricing and current lead times;
- compare approved colourways and sizes;
- submit a room brief;
- create a quote request;
- retrieve a product’s Pramāṇa record;
- request a custom adaptation within allowed boundaries.

For acoustic and architectural products, add:

- assembly drawings;
- mounting details;
- tested performance values;
- fire and care documents;
- replacement-face and repair instructions.

### 6.5 CRM and private-client relationship system

CRM records should capture relationships, not just email addresses:

- design vocabulary and client fit;
- project type and estimated completion date;
- sample preferences;
- price sensitivity and approval structure;
- previous purchases and room locations;
- referrals and introductions;
- consent for communication and photography;
- next action and owner inside the Aitihya team.

The CRM should ensure that the relationship gradually belongs to the company, while preserving appropriate credit and trust for Anjan’s introductions.

### 6.6 Data governance and security

Pramāṇa must separate public storytelling from private supply-chain information.

- Public: approved region, technique, maker attribution, material, care and film.
- Trade-only: detailed specifications, installation, test reports, lead times and samples.
- Internal: exact coordinates, private contracts, costs, payment records, capacity and restricted cultural information.
- Owner-only: name, gifting message, room photographs, transfer and service history.

Use role-based access, backups, audit logs, consent records, media-rights expiry and a documented deletion/retention policy. Have legal counsel review privacy, consumer, advertising, data-transfer and documentary-consent requirements across India and export markets.

## 9. Acoustic and technical textile opportunities

### 9.1 What we can honestly claim

Use the term **sound absorption** or **reverberation reduction**, not “soundproofing,” unless the complete building assembly has been tested for sound transmission loss.

A textile surface can reduce reflections and reverberation inside a room. It does not automatically stop sound travelling through a wall, floor or door.

### 9.2 Acoustic product roadmap

#### Phase 1: decorative acoustic wall panel

Structure:

- replaceable Aitihya textile face;
- acoustic core supplied by a specialist partner, such as recycled PET or mineral-fibre board;
- concealed or crafted timber/aluminium frame;
- rear mounting system;
- removable face for repair or conservation;
- NFC/QR on the reverse, not inside the acoustic core.

Use the textile as the visible, replaceable surface and partner for the engineered acoustic assembly. This protects Aitihya from becoming a panel manufacturer before it has testing capability.

#### Phase 2: suspended textile baffle

For hospitality, private libraries, dining rooms and media rooms:

- lightweight framed or quilted baffles;
- ceiling suspension kit supplied by a qualified installer;
- repeatable module sizes;
- documented cleaning and replacement procedure.

Do not launch this until the first wall-panel system has passed testing and installation trials.

### 9.3 Required performance work

Test the **complete assembly**, not only the decorative textile face.

- Sound absorption: ASTM C423 or ISO 354; publish the test setup, thickness, air gap and result.
- Fire/surface burning: select the market-appropriate test, such as ASTM E84 for US applications; do not call a product fire-rated from a fibre description alone.
- Lightfastness, colourfastness, abrasion, seam strength, dimensional stability and cleaning compatibility according to end use.
- Structural load and fixing tests for wall-mounted or suspended systems.

ASTM C423 and ISO 354 describe laboratory approaches for measuring sound absorption, while ASTM E84 measures comparative flame spread and smoke characteristics for exposed building surfaces. These standards do not turn an untested textile into a compliant architectural system. [ASTM C423](https://store.astm.org/standards/c423), [ISO 354](https://www.iso.org/standard/34545.html), [ASTM E84](https://store.astm.org/e0084-20.html)

### 9.4 Acoustic product commercial offer

Sell acoustic panels as a complete specification service:

1. room brief and reverberation problem;
2. design and size selection;
3. textile and colour approval;
4. acoustic assembly specification;
5. test report and fire documentation;
6. production and installation;
7. care, replacement-face and repair service.

The acoustic line can become a Year 2–3 commercial extension because it gives designers a functional reason to specify Aitihya beyond decoration.

## 10. Supply chain and production system

### 10.1 Core-and-accent guild model

Use the useful insight from Plans 2 and 3, but keep cultural control with the relevant makers:

1. A capable base guild produces repeatable ground cloth or supporting components where appropriate.
2. Master artisans apply the culturally specific structure, border, intervention or finishing that makes the piece distinctive.
3. A finishing partner handles blocking, lining, mounting, framing and packing to an approved standard.
4. Aitihya owns the product record, customer experience and design system—not the traditional knowledge itself. Pramāṇa governs the evidence record.

This model is acceptable only when the proposed division of labour is agreed with the artisans and does not falsely market a partially machine-made product as entirely made by a master artisan.

### 10.2 Partner agreements

Each workshop agreement should cover:

- technique and work scope;
- pricing and payment timing;
- advances and cancellation terms;
- capacity reservation, not coercive exclusivity;
- quality expectations and acceptable variation;
- attribution and photography consent;
- use of names, stories, motifs and designs;
- confidentiality where requested;
- repair/rework responsibility;
- safety and working conditions;
- benefit-sharing or community contribution;
- exit and relationship handover.

WIPO’s guidance on traditional cultural expressions emphasises consent, respectful engagement, attribution and equitable collaboration. Build those principles into contracts and decision-making, rather than treating them as later marketing language. [WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/traditional-knowledge/fashion)

### 10.3 Quality system

For every SKU maintain:

- approved reference sample;
- tolerance chart for dimensions, colour and texture;
- fibre/yarn/dye record;
- production time and capacity estimate;
- QC checklist at workshop, finishing and dispatch;
- photographs before and after finishing;
- defect and repair log;
- customer acceptance record;
- care and packaging instructions.

The brand promise should include **honest variation**. Define what variation is beautiful and acceptable, and what is a defect requiring repair or rejection.

### 10.4 Finishing and conservation

Create or appoint a finishing atelier for:

- washing or stabilisation where appropriate;
- blocking and dimensional correction;
- lining and backing;
- hand stitching and edge finishing;
- framing and mounting;
- archival tissue and protective packaging;
- condition reports and conservation recommendations.

For rare textiles, include a repair and conservation service from launch. This creates trust, repeat contact and a long-term ownership relationship.

## 12. Top five competitors and our differentiation

### 1. Good Earth — India

**They own:** broad Indian design-led luxury lifestyle, home, décor, textiles, retail and international reach.

**Why they matter:** They already teach wealthy customers to buy Indian craft through a coherent design language and established retail experience.

**Our difference:** Aitihya is narrower, more textile-specialist and more collector-oriented. We should offer fewer designs, deeper material records, rarer techniques, numbered works, designer specification support and stronger maker-level provenance. We should not try to match Good Earth’s breadth or store network. [Good Earth home textiles](https://www.goodearth.in/catalogue/category/living/bed-bath_194/?category_shop=Home+%3E+Home+Textiles+%3E+Cushion+Covers)

### 2. OBEETEE — India/global

**They own:** a century-scale Indian rug platform, design collaborations, custom rugs and luxury hospitality/residential project capability.

**Why they matter:** They prove Indian craft can serve international designers and large projects with strong production infrastructure.

**Our difference:** Do not compete on rugs. Own the non-floor textile space: wall textiles, throws, coverlets and culturally specific textile structures. Build a smaller, more anthropologically documented house with a softer, more intimate product experience. OBEETEE’s public materials show the strength of its rug, custom and hospitality platform. [OBEETEE projects](https://obeetee.com/trade/Oberoi_Delhi.aspx), [OBEETEE collections](https://obeetee.com/trade/collectionHome.aspx?tab=0)

### 3. Jaipur Rugs — India/global

**They own:** handmade rugs, large artisan-network infrastructure, designer trade and international distribution.

**Why they matter:** They are a major scale and channel benchmark for converting artisan production into a global interior brand.

**Our difference:** Be materially narrower and higher-touch. Do not become a mass-to-luxury rug catalogue. Focus on textile forms that are intimate, wall-based, portable or room-layering, with fewer editions and stronger research provenance. [Jaipur Rugs trade](https://trade.jaipurrugs.com/)

### 4. de Gournay — UK/global

**They own:** hand-painted wallpapers, hand-embroidered wallcoverings and bespoke fabric surfaces sold through luxury interiors.

**Why they matter:** They demonstrate the power of a craft-led, highly bespoke, design-professional channel. They also offer protective, stain-repellent and flame-retardant finishes in selected products.

**Our difference:** Do not imitate pictorial wallpaper. Own the structural textile: weave, fibre, loom, hand-spun material and living regional technique. Our signature should be tactile and architectural without becoming a de Gournay-style scenic wall. [de Gournay collections](https://degournay.com/wallpaper), [de Gournay fabrics](https://degournay.com/design-collections/fabric/hand-painted-fabrics)

### 5. The Rug Company — UK/global

**They own:** designer collaborations, global showrooms, bespoke rugs, trade accounts, sample libraries and project/installation support.

**Why they matter:** They are an excellent model for a specifier-friendly luxury craft platform.

**Our difference:** Apply the same trade discipline to a new category of Indian textile surfaces rather than competing in floor coverings. Build an “Aitihya Material Library” with samples, technical data, lead times, custom sizing rules and installation support. [The Rug Company trade programme](https://uk.therugcompany.com/trade-programme/), [custom/bespoke](https://www.therugcompany.com/custom-bespoke/)

### Competitive positioning

> **Good Earth has breadth. OBEETEE and Jaipur Rugs have scale. de Gournay has bespoke surface drama. The Rug Company has trade infrastructure. Aitihya should own contemporary Indian textile knowledge translated into a tightly edited, documented and technically dependable interior system.**

Do not claim that this category has no competitors. The opportunity is a sharper combination of attributes, not an empty market.

### 13.5 Marketing and branding strategy

#### The decision on social media

**Yes, Aitihya should use social media—but as a curated editorial showroom and trust layer, not as its primary sales channel.**

The target customer is reached through a chain of confidence:

```text
Visual discovery
      ↓
Material and documentary proof
      ↓
Designer/private-client enquiry
      ↓
Physical sample or appointment
      ↓
Quote and deposit
      ↓
Installation, ownership and referral
```

Social media should create desire, recognition and permission to start a relationship. The website, trade portal, private appointment, sample library and Pramāṇa Story Page should do the serious conversion work.

Do not judge the strategy by follower count. Judge it by qualified designer introductions, sample requests, appointments, deposits, repeat orders and referrals.

#### Brand character

Aitihya should feel:

- quiet rather than loud;
- tactile rather than glossy;
- contemporary rather than folkloric;
- generous rather than promotional;
- precise rather than over-explained;
- human without using hardship as decoration.

Visual direction:

- architectural spaces and generous negative space;
- close macro views of fibre, tension, edge and handwork;
- ecru, bone, charcoal, slate, madder, mineral and natural-dye colours;
- natural or controlled directional light;
- slow, deliberate camera movement;
- restrained typography and captions;
- product first, maker and place with consent;
- no luxury-car, champagne, influencer or “exotic India” shorthand.

Copy rules:

- say exactly what is known and what is inferred;
- explain variation as part of hand production, not as a defence for poor quality;
- avoid “the last artisan,” “ancient secret,” “saving a dying craft” and other claims that cannot be substantiated;
- name the maker or atelier where authorised;
- distinguish traditional cultural knowledge from Aitihya’s new design contribution;
- connect every story to the object and the room in which it will live.

- Create a list of every craft/fibre relationship Anjan considers commercially viable.
- Establish the craft scorecard and cultural-consent checklist.

#### Week 2 — supply and rights

- Visit or call the top candidate workshops.
- Record capacity, lead time, minimum quantities, payment expectations and current quality.
- Draft workshop and co-development agreements.
- Decide what information can be public, trade-only or internal.
- Start trademark and domain clearance for the chosen brand name.

**Deliverables:** shortlist of two core craft families plus one halo technique; draft agreements; provenance permissions matrix.

#### Week 3 — product briefs

- Write briefs for 12 initial designs.
- Define dimensions, materials, palette, finishing, price hypothesis and target room.
- Select master samples and acceptable handmade variation.
- Choose the finishing/framing partner.
- Decide which products receive QR, standard NFC or secure NFC.

**Deliverables:** 12 product briefs, first BOMs, technology/tag decision, prototype calendar.

#### Week 4 — buyer discovery

- Conduct 15 Indian designer interviews.
- Conduct 5 UAE/UK/US designer interviews.
- Conduct 10 private-buyer interviews.
- Ask about actual recent purchases, not hypothetical interest.
- Test the price bands and lead-time promises.

**Deliverables:** interview synthesis, revised product briefs, top-20 account priority list, objections log.

### Month 2: prototype and make the brand specifiable

#### Week 5 — first prototypes

- Start 8–12 prototypes across the selected workshops.
- Build the internal product database.
- Write the provenance schema and consented maker records.
- Photograph raw materials and process steps.

**Deliverables:** production underway, provenance MVP, product ID structure, first cost cards.

#### Week 6 — quality and technology

- Check dimensions, shrinkage, colour, handle, seams, backing and finish.
- Test care assumptions on representative samples.
- Embed or attach tags to sample products.
- Validate QR/NFC scan experience on current iOS and Android devices.
- Create a first acoustic-panel mockup only if a technical partner is available.

**Deliverables:** QC checklist, revised BOMs, working product pages, tag prototype, acoustic feasibility decision.

#### Week 7 — trade toolkit

- Build the physical “Aitihya Material Library.”
- Prepare trade price list, recommended retail price and commission rules.
- Prepare lead-time matrix, room images, specification sheets and care cards.
- Create private designer portal or password-protected folder.
- Write the first three founder/field-note stories.

**Deliverables:** trade kit v1, digital trade folder, sales scripts, product photography brief.

#### Week 8 — cost and refine

- Receive and critique prototypes.
- Run a construction and finishing review with the artisans.
- Reject weak designs early.
- Recalculate unit economics after actual production time and rework.
- Confirm the final 6–8 core and 3–4 edition designs for the first presentation.

**Deliverables:** final presentation range, contribution model, prototype rejection log, sample loan policy.

### Month 3: sell and decide whether to scale

#### Week 9 — India trade launch

- Host an appointment-only presentation in Mumbai or Delhi.
- Invite target designers, private clients and one or two cultural/design partners.
- Offer sample loans and specification meetings.
- Take deposits for approved products and paid development fees for bespoke work.

**Deliverables:** first briefs, first deposits, account-by-account next actions.

#### Week 10 — project conversion

- Visit or video-call priority designers.
- Produce room-specific mockups for no more than five serious opportunities.
- Quote using trade terms, realistic lead times and deposit rules.
- Start one pilot production order.

**Deliverables:** five live quotes, one or more production orders, first project case-study candidate.

#### Week 11 — export pilot

- Present in Dubai or run a partner-led UAE preview.
- Ship only products with completed origin, fibre, care and landed-cost files.
- Test packaging, insurance, customs paperwork and delivery condition.
- Collect designer feedback on price, colour and dimensions.

**Deliverables:** two export trials or a documented failure analysis; landed-cost sheet; packaging improvements.

#### Week 12 — review and scale gate

- Review deposits, conversion, objections, margin, production time and claims.
- Identify the three best-selling or most requested designs.
- Decide which products to replenish, redesign or kill.
- Sign only the workshop agreements justified by paid demand.
- Set the next six-month hiring and capital plan.

**Scale gate:** proceed only if the business has at least five paid pilots, three unrelated buyers, two export trials or a clear reason one market should be delayed, and positive contribution on the repeatable core range.

## 16. Main risks and controls

| Risk | Control |
|---|---|
| Founder dependence | Document relationships; hire product/operations lead; create account ownership |
| Artisan bottleneck | Core-and-accent model; multiple partners; capacity planning; no false exclusivity |
| Cultural appropriation or community conflict | Consent, attribution, benefit-sharing, maker approval and restricted information |
| Inconsistent quality | Master samples, tolerances, multi-stage QC and repair/rework budget |
| Margin illusion | Product-level contribution accounting including trade and fulfilment costs |
| Export failure | SKU-level classification, labels, care, origin, insurance and landed-cost file |
| Technology becoming theatre | QR/NFC only where it serves authenticity, care, service or data |
| Acoustic liability | Specialist partner, complete-assembly testing and conservative claims |
| Luxury dilution | Small assortment, no discount-led growth, no broad marketplace listing |
| Slow cash conversion | Deposits, capped bespoke work, limited finished stock and weekly cash reporting |

> **Aitihya should become the most trusted contemporary Indian textile house for designers specifying exceptional homes: a small, repeatable product system with a Pramāṇa-backed provenance layer, technical credibility and a human craft network that can scale without being erased.**
