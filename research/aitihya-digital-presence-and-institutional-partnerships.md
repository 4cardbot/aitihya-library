# Aitihya digital presence and institutional partnerships

**Prepared:** 25 October 2026  
**Brand:** Aitihya  
**Evidence and provenance layer:** Pramāṇa  
**Purpose:** turn Anjan’s existing archive of artisan photographs, videos and field knowledge into a high-trust digital presence, while using government, NGO, cultural and CSR relationships to support craft continuity without diluting Aitihya’s luxury positioning.

## 1. Executive decision

Yes, we should start publishing now, before Collection 01 is fully ready. The archive is not merely marketing material; it is the beginning of Aitihya’s authority.

But we should not publish it as a stream of disconnected artisan portraits or as a charity campaign. The public should gradually understand:

1. Anjan has unusual field knowledge and long-term relationships.
2. The artisans are skilled authors and makers, not background characters.
3. Aitihya is translating living textile knowledge into exceptional contemporary objects.
4. The finished objects are desirable in their own right.
5. Pramāṇa gives the objects evidence, provenance, care and accountability.

Government and NGO relationships are valuable, but they should initially serve three purposes:

- fund or enable documentation, training, design development and craft continuity;
- create credible institutional commissions, exhibitions and case studies;
- introduce Aitihya to serious designers, collectors, cultural institutions and CSR decision-makers.

They should not become the main sales channel or force Aitihya to compete primarily on low price, tenders or “support local artisans” sentiment.

## 2. The two-engine model

Build two related but clearly separated engines.

| Engine | Audience | Primary offer | Success measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aitihya brand engine | Interior designers, architects, collectors, private clients | Exceptional cushions, throws, wall textiles and selective bespoke commissions | Qualified enquiries, sample requests, deposits, repeat specifications and full-margin sales |
| Institutional continuity engine | Government agencies, museums, cultural bodies, NGOs, CSR foundations and mission-led companies | Documentation, training, design intervention, exhibitions, commissioned objects and measurable craft-continuity programmes | Paid projects, artisan income, documented skills, new apprentices, responsible visibility and credible case studies |

The two engines may share films, research and provenance infrastructure, but they should not share every product, price or message.

### Brand language

- **Aitihya:** contemporary Indian textile objects for exceptional rooms and collectors.
- **Pramāṇa:** the evidence layer that records what an object is, where and how it was made, who contributed, what is verified, and how it should be cared for.
- **Institutional programme:** use a descriptive working name such as **Aitihya Craft Continuity Programme** or **Aitihya Field Archive** only when speaking to institutions. Do not create a second consumer brand prematurely.

## 3. What to do with the existing archive

### 3.1 Do not release the archive all at once

An archive creates authority when it is edited. A large undifferentiated folder of images creates noise and may expose people or knowledge without context.

Organise the material into five controlled libraries:

1. **Maker library:** portraits, interviews, permissions, names, preferred attribution and approved biographical details.
2. **Process library:** raw materials, tools, stages, gestures, sound, defects, repair and finishing.
3. **Place library:** landscape, architecture and working environments, showing only the location detail each person has approved.
4. **Object library:** existing products, prototypes, details, dimensions, use and condition.
5. **Field-notes library:** Anjan’s observations, historical context, terminology, translations and research questions.

Each asset should have a simple internal record:

- file name and date;
- maker/workshop/community attribution;
- location at an agreed level of precision;
- who owns the image or video rights;
- consent status and permitted use;
- language and translation status;
- whether it can be used publicly, privately, commercially or only for research;
- whether the person has approved the caption;
- any safety, privacy or cultural restrictions.

This becomes part of the Pramāṇa operating library, even when the asset itself is not published.

### 3.2 Consent and dignity rules

Before using existing content in a campaign, confirm consent retroactively where possible and establish a forward-looking process for every new shoot.

The release should be available in a language the contributor understands and should state:

- who Aitihya is;
- where the film or photograph may appear;
- whether it may be used for sales, fundraising, press, education or advertising;
- whether the contributor will be named or remain anonymous;
- whether payment, a copy, credit or another benefit is provided;
- whether the contributor can request corrections or restrictions;
- how long the permission lasts and how withdrawal will work;
- whether the image includes other people, children, private homes or sacred practices.

Do not use poverty, remoteness or “dying village” imagery as the emotional hook. Show skill, concentration, decision-making, humour, work conditions, material intelligence and the makers’ own ambitions. Do not publish precise home locations, personal phone numbers or continuous location data without a strong, documented reason.

Traditional cultural expressions should not be treated as Aitihya-owned intellectual property by default. Distinguish inherited community knowledge from new Aitihya co-developed designs, and document attribution, permission, compensation and benefit-sharing.

## 4. Digital presence architecture

### 4.1 Website: the trust and conversion base

The website should be the durable home of the story. Social platforms should lead people to it, not replace it.

The minimum public structure is:

1. **Aitihya:** one-sentence proposition, strongest room image, one short film and a private-preview CTA.
2. **Collection 01:** the six cushions, three throws/coverlets and three wall panels as a small, coherent world.
3. **Field journal:** edited stories about material, place, process and the people who choose how the work evolves.
4. **Pramāṇa:** explain the record, certificate classes, evidence boundaries, care and repair.
5. **For designers:** downloadable trade introduction, dimensions, materials, lead times, sample request and specification contact.
6. **Institutional partnerships:** documentation, exhibitions, commissions, craft-continuity programmes and contact for cultural/CSR partners.
7. **Private preview:** a low-friction form for qualified buyers to request the catalogue, appointment or sample chest.

Every product page should eventually include:

- strong object photography before the long story;
- dimensions, material, care and lead time;
- the maker/process story with consent;
- edition and availability;
- Pramāṇa record or “record in preparation” status;
- a private enquiry CTA rather than an unqualified discount or mass-sale message.

### 4.2 Channel roles

Do not treat every platform as a duplicate feed.

| Channel | Job | Content | Primary call to action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instagram | Visual discovery and trust | Short process films, object details, maker-led narratives, room applications and founder field notes | Save, share, request private preview or trade pack |
| Pinterest | Long-tail interior discovery | Vertical room images, material details, panel scale, colour stories and links back to product/journal pages | Visit the relevant collection or specification page |
| LinkedIn | Institutional and professional credibility | Partnership announcements, research, craft-continuity outcomes, design-trade insights and founder perspective | Introduce a designer, CSR lead, museum or cultural institution |
| YouTube or Vimeo | Cinematic depth | 3–8 minute documentaries, process films and collection films | Watch the full story and request a conversation |
| Email | Owned relationship | Monthly field letter, private previews, new samples, events and institutional updates | Reply, book an appointment or request materials |
| Website journal | Searchable memory and authority | Edited long-form field stories, product essays and Pramāṇa explainers | Explore a collection, commission or partnership |

### 4.3 The first audience is not “everyone interested in craft”

Build a permission-based list of specific people:

- 100 Indian interior designers and architects who work on high-value residential or hospitality projects;
- 25 international designers or representatives across the US, UK and UAE;
- 25 collectors, art advisers and design-led private clients;
- 20 cultural institutions, museums, foundations or curators;
- 20 CSR, sustainability or corporate-foundation leads;
- 10 carefully selected journalists, editors and documentary collaborators.

This is a relationship map, not a target to spam. Every person should have a reason for being included, an owner inside the Aitihya team and a next action.

## 5. Content system for the first 90 days

### 5.1 Content pillars

Use six repeatable series so the archive becomes a recognisable editorial world.

#### 1. Hands at work

15–45 second videos of a specific action: preparing fibre, setting a loom, choosing a colour, stitching an edge, correcting a mistake or finishing a surface. Always name the maker where approved.

#### 2. Material intelligence

Close-ups and short explanations of fibre, yarn, dye, texture, sound, weight, drape, variation and care. This builds luxury credibility better than generic “handmade” copy.

#### 3. Field notes

Anjan explains one observation, word, technique, place or unresolved question in plain language. Keep the anthropology precise but accessible.

#### 4. From field to room

Show how a material or process becomes a cushion, throw or wall panel in a contemporary interior. This is the bridge from cultural interest to purchase intent.

#### 5. The maker’s point of view

Ask what the maker wants to change, preserve, teach or make next. Do not make every interview about Aitihya’s mission.

#### 6. Proof of care

Show sample approval, fair payment processes, training, repair, attribution, edition records, packaging and quality control. This is where Pramāṇa becomes credible.

### 5.2 Weekly cadence during the first two months

Start with a manageable rhythm, not a content factory:

- 2 short process videos per week;
- 1 finished-object or material post per week;
- 1 field-note or maker-led carousel per week;
- 3–5 days of light Stories, only when there is something meaningful to show;
- 1 LinkedIn post per week for professional or institutional audiences;
- 1 email every two weeks during the private-preview period;
- 1 longer film or edited journal story every four weeks.

One well-edited 5-minute field film can produce a trailer, three process clips, a material carousel, a still-image story, a journal article and an email. Build a rights-cleared content package at the time of filming.

### 5.3 Sequence the launch as a story arc

#### Weeks 1–2: establish the point of view

- Publish the Aitihya proposition and a founder field note.
- Release one maker/process story without selling a product.
- Put a private-preview form on the website.
- Invite 30 trusted designers and cultural contacts to an unlisted preview page.

#### Weeks 3–4: introduce the material world

- Publish three material studies.
- Show the same technique at different stages.
- Release the first short collection film.
- Begin collecting designer questions about dimensions, lead times, care and specification.

#### Weeks 5–6: reveal the object

- Introduce the first two cushions and one throw or panel.
- Show the connection between the field source, the design decision and the finished object.
- Send a private digital trade pack to qualified designers.

#### Weeks 7–8: demonstrate proof

- Explain Pramāṇa in plain language.
- Show sample approval, maker attribution, variation and care.
- Hold a small private digital or physical preview.
- Ask for sample requests and deposits, not generic feedback.

#### Weeks 9–12: create conversion and institutional momentum

- Publish a complete Collection 01 story sequence.
- Announce one carefully chosen institutional or NGO collaboration only if there is a signed scope and consent.
- Hold a designer presentation or salon.
- Follow up personally with every qualified lead.
- Publish a short impact and process note with what happened, what was paid and what will happen next.

## 6. The conversion funnel

The content should move people through a deliberate sequence:

```text
Archive content
    → field story or material insight
    → Aitihya collection / journal page
    → private preview or trade-pack request
    → conversation and sample
    → deposit / commission / institutional brief
    → Pramāṇa record and post-sale story
```

Use different forms for different audiences:

- **Private client:** preferred object, room/project type, country, appointment request.
- **Designer:** studio name, project stage, required dimensions, client location, sample needs and deadline.
- **Institutional/CSR:** organisation, craft geography, programme objective, funding route, procurement constraints and decision date.

Do not ask every person to “shop the collection” before the products, pricing and fulfilment are ready. Early CTAs should be **request the private preview**, **request a sample chest**, **discuss a commission** or **explore a partnership**.

## 7. Government, NGO and CSR strategy

### 7.1 Our answer: yes, but not as the first core buyer

Selling to governments and NGOs can build credibility and support revival, but it is not automatically a good luxury-business channel. Institutional procurement can involve slower decisions, formal specifications, lower price ceilings, purchase-order requirements and reporting obligations.

Use institutions to create **proof, capability and funded continuity**. Use private clients and designers to create **margin, desirability and repeat demand**.

### 7.2 The best institutional offers

#### A. Paid documentation commission

An institution, museum, foundation or CSR partner pays Aitihya and approved collaborators to document a craft practice responsibly.

Possible outputs:

- 3–5 short films;
- a consented maker and process archive;
- a bilingual or multilingual digital field guide;
- material samples and technical records;
- a small, clearly attributed prototype collection;
- a public exhibition, screening or digital archive;
- Pramāṇa records for the documented objects.

This is more valuable than asking an NGO to buy luxury cushions it may not need.

#### B. Craft-continuity fellowship

A company foundation or cultural institution funds a 6–12 month programme with a defined group of makers.

Possible outputs:

- paid apprenticeships;
- tools or work-environment improvements decided with the makers;
- design-development time;
- agreed minimum purchase commitments;
- product testing and quality training;
- documentation and public storytelling;
- a transparent impact report.

The sponsor should fund the programme, not use the artisans’ stories as free advertising.

#### C. Institutional collection or commission

Sell a small number of finished works to:

- museums or cultural institutions;
- embassy or consular residences where appropriate;
- public cultural buildings;
- design-led hotels and members’ clubs;
- corporate art and heritage collections;
- government or institutional gifting programmes.

Use a distinct institutional specification and price, while keeping the strongest Collection 01 pieces scarce. A public commission should still have Aitihya design authorship, full attribution and a Pramāṇa record.

#### D. Designer and programme partner

Partner with an NGO, producer collective or government-linked cluster that already has community legitimacy. Aitihya contributes design direction, documentation, market access and quality systems; the partner contributes local trust, governance and programme capability.

Do not make Aitihya the owner of the community’s tradition or position Anjan as the saviour of a craft.

### 7.3 Government routes worth exploring

The Office of the Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, is the Government of India’s nodal agency for the handicrafts sector and implements the National Handicrafts Development Programme and cluster-development schemes. Its current portal includes routes for NGO registration/empanelment and designer empanelment. Start with the relevant regional office and scheme requirements rather than sending a generic brand deck. [Development Commissioner (Handicrafts)](https://handicrafts.nic.in/) and [official schemes](https://handicrafts.nic.in/schemes.aspx).

The Ministry of MSME’s SFURTI scheme is designed around artisan and traditional-industry collectives and can support infrastructure, training, product development, design intervention, packaging, marketing and common facilities. It is generally a cluster/implementing-agency route, not a simple Aitihya grant application. Explore whether an existing NGO, cluster or implementing agency connected to the artisans is eligible to lead a proposal. [SFURTI](https://sfurti.msme.gov.in/) and [revised SFURTI guidelines](https://sfurti.msme.gov.in/WriteReadData/Circular/SFURTI_NEW.pdf).

Government e-Marketplace (GeM) is a procurement route for government departments, PSUs and affiliated buyers. It allows eligible sellers/service providers to register, list products and participate in procurement opportunities. Register only after Aitihya has a stable legal entity, tax and bank documentation, standardised specifications, realistic lead times, warranty/care language and a product line suitable for institutional purchasing. [Government e-Marketplace overview](https://services.india.gov.in/service/detail/government-e-marketplace-gem) and [GeM seller manual](https://assets-bg.gem.gov.in/resources/pdf/seller-user-manual.pdf).

### 7.4 CSR route

The Ministry of Corporate Affairs’ CSR FAQ states that Schedule VII activities are interpreted broadly and includes areas such as protection of national heritage, art and culture, and promotion/development of traditional arts and handicrafts. It also clarifies that CSR expenditure must follow the permitted routes and that an in-kind contribution cannot simply be monetised as CSR expenditure. This means Aitihya should pitch a defined programme with an eligible implementing structure, budget, outputs and reporting—not “buy our luxury products to support artisans.” [MCA CSR FAQ](https://www.mca.gov.in/Ministry/pdf/FAQ_CSR.pdf).

Have a company secretary, chartered accountant or qualified CSR adviser review the final structure before promising CSR eligibility, tax treatment or impact claims.

## 8. Institutional outreach sequence

### First 30 days

- Make a list of 20 warm institutional relationships.
- Classify each as buyer, funder, introducer, programme partner or media/cultural amplifier.
- Ask what they are already mandated or funded to do; do not start with Aitihya’s product catalogue.
- Select three possible pilot partners: one cultural institution, one NGO/cluster partner and one corporate/CSR foundation.
- Prepare a six-page institutional brief: problem, craft context, proposed intervention, outputs, budget range, governance and next step.

### Days 31–60

- Hold discovery conversations with each partner.
- Choose one paid documentation or programme pilot and one institutional collection/commission prospect.
- Confirm artisan consent, partner responsibilities, payment schedule, image rights and public claims.
- Build a measurement baseline before filming or training.

### Days 61–90

- Sign one pilot scope and obtain an advance.
- Produce one short, consented case-study film.
- Develop one institutional product or exhibition proposal.
- Document what changed for makers, the partner and the audience.
- Publish only after all parties approve the credits, images and claims.

## 9. What to measure

### Brand metrics

- qualified designer/trade requests;
- private-preview signups by source;
- sample requests;
- email replies and appointment bookings;
- deposits and conversion rate;
- average realised price and discount rate;
- repeat specification or referral;
- saves, shares and completion rate on process films.

### Institutional metrics

- artisan payment and payment timeliness;
- number of makers with approved attribution records;
- apprentices or skills transferred;
- new or repeat purchase commitments;
- prototypes completed and accepted;
- documentation assets with valid consent;
- programme cost per maker or per output;
- partner satisfaction and renewal;
- claims that can be evidenced through Pramāṇa.

Do not use follower count, video views or a government logo as proof of commercial traction. They are useful only when they lead to qualified relationships, paid work or accountable impact.

## 10. Guardrails

- Keep Aitihya’s luxury collection, institutional editions and CSR-funded work in separate cost centres and product records.
- Never discount Collection 01 heavily in exchange for a logo, introduction or vague promise of publicity.
- Do not publish a maker’s name, face, location or story without approved consent and attribution.
- Do not claim “revival,” “fair trade,” “sustainable,” “endangered,” “zero-waste” or “community-owned” without an evidence standard.
- Do not use a government or NGO relationship as implied endorsement of the entire Aitihya brand unless written permission allows it.
- Keep a written separation between a grant, donation, purchase order, sponsorship, commission and CSR project.
- Pay artisans for development, sampling, photography, interviews and rework—not only for the final saleable object.
- Avoid creating demand that the workshop cannot fulfil.
- Protect private cultural knowledge and sacred practices from content extraction.

## 11. Immediate recommendation

Start with one 90-day public editorial season called **“Before the Object”** or **“Field to Form”**:

- 12–15 edited short videos;
- 3 longer maker/process stories;
- 3 material essays;
- 6 founder field notes;
- 3 product reveals;
- 1 Pramāṇa explainer;
- 1 private designer preview;
- 1 institutional pilot proposal;
- 1 consented case-study story only after a paid collaboration exists.

At the same time, pursue one institutional pilot with a defined budget and deliverables. The strongest first partner is likely to be an NGO, cluster organisation, cultural institution or CSR foundation that can fund documentation and capacity-building—not a government department asking for a low-cost bulk catalogue.

The strategic sequence is:

```text
Existing archive
    → consent and editorial system
    → high-trust digital presence
    → designer and collector relationships
    → paid Collection 01 demand
    → one accountable institutional pilot
    → documented case study and stronger trust
    → repeat private and institutional commissions
```

Aitihya should use institutions to make craft continuity more possible, not to make the brand look virtuous. The brand becomes trusted when the people, objects, prices, evidence and outcomes all hold together.
