# Venture thesis research sources: premium Indian textile / home / lifestyle brand

**Research cut-off:** 26 September 2026  
**Scope:** Evidence relevant to a premium Indian textile, home, or lifestyle brand serving India's wealthiest consumers and exporting to the US, UK, UAE and other markets, with a possible 3–4 year exit.  
**Method:** High-trust primary sources were preferred: government datasets and rules, official trade bodies, official company reports, and first-party luxury-market research. “Implication” notes below are bounded interpretations of the evidence, not recommendations or strategic decisions.

## 1. Wealth, luxury demand and customer expectations

### Global wealth concentration

**Source:** Capgemini Research Institute, *World Wealth Report 2026*, press release dated **4 June 2026**.

The report defines HNWIs as people with at least $1M of investable assets, excluding their primary residence, collectibles, consumables and consumer durables. It reports:

- global HNWI wealth of $98.3T at year-end 2025, up 8.7%;
- a global UHNWI population of roughly 250,000, up 9.4%;
- the top 1% of HNWIs holding 34.8% of HNWI wealth;
- India’s HNWI population increasing by 11,300 in 2025;
- the US adding 736,000 HNWIs and reaching 8.7M, while the UK’s HNWI population grew 2.6%; and
- Middle East HNWI population declining 1.4% in 2025 in the report’s regional model.

**Implication:** A “top 1%” customer definition may be economically powerful but operationally narrow. The report supports testing specific wealth/lifestyle cohorts rather than assuming that all affluent consumers behave alike. It also supports treating the US and UK as distinct customer and logistics markets, not one generic export market.

**Caveat:** This is wealth data, not demand data for textiles, home goods or Indian craft. It does not identify the number of Indian consumers who would buy a particular product at a particular price.

Source: <https://www.capgemini.com/gb-en/news/press-releases/global-millionaire-population-jumps-by-nearly-2-million-in-2025-driven-by-strong-stock-market-performance-worldwide/> (published 4 June 2026; accessed 26 September 2026).

### The highest-end luxury segment

**Source:** Altagamma, *True-Luxury Global Consumer Insight 2025*, public summary dated **8 July 2025**.

Altagamma states that the highest-end customers account for only 0.1% of the total but 37% of market value. The public summary says these consumers prioritise experiences and expect personalisation, intimacy, excellence and renewed attention to wellbeing from brands.

**Implication:** The evidence points to a potential difference between a premium product business and a true luxury relationship: provenance, private access, service, customisation and after-sales may matter alongside the textile or object.

**Caveat:** The public summary does not provide enough detail to transfer the 0.1% / 37% ratio directly to India, home textiles or artisan goods.

Source: <https://altagamma.it/en/studi-e-ricerche/> (entry dated 8 July 2025; accessed 26 September 2026).

### Luxury market direction and regional differences

**Source:** Altagamma, *Altagamma Bain Monitor Update June 2026*, dated **25 June 2026**.

The public summary forecasts up to 2% growth for global luxury in 2026 and 2–4% growth for personal luxury goods. It says luxury experiences and assets such as hospitality, restaurants, megayachts, private jets and art drive growth at about 4%. For personal luxury goods, it identifies the Americas as the growth driver, Europe and the Middle East as declining, and China as in cautious recovery. It also says artificial intelligence is increasingly decisive in purchasing choices.

**Implication:** The export thesis needs to separate demand for personal luxury goods from demand for experiences, design and hospitality-adjacent products. A textile/home brand may be exposed to more than one of these demand pools, but the source does not establish which one is most attractive.

**Caveat:** This is a global luxury monitor. It is a directional market signal, not a forecast for an Indian craft brand or a UAE-specific customer segment.

Source: <https://altagamma.it/en/studi-e-ricerche/> (entry dated 25 June 2026; accessed 26 September 2026).

### India’s visible ultra-wealth population

**Source:** Hurun Research Institute, *Hurun Global Rich List 2025*, issued **27 March 2025**; wealth snapshot **15 January 2025**.

Hurun reports 284 US-dollar billionaires in India, placing India third globally, and identifies Mumbai as home to 90 billionaires. It reports that Indian billionaires are concentrated in sectors including healthcare, consumer goods and industrial products.

**Implication:** India has a large and visible pool of ultra-wealthy households and business networks that may be relevant to relationship-led selling, gifting, interiors and private-client access.

**Caveat:** Billionaire counts are not a proxy for the broader top 1% of Indian wealth, household purchasing behaviour, or willingness to buy craft products. Hurun’s methodology and wealth estimates should not be mixed with Capgemini’s investable-asset definition.

Source: <https://www.hurun.net/en-us/info/detail?num=2QJ1CKW4OBC4> (issued 27 March 2025; wealth snapshot 15 January 2025; accessed 26 September 2026).

## 2. Indian handloom and craft supply base

### Scale of the handloom ecosystem

**Source:** Office of the Development Commissioner (Handlooms), Ministry of Textiles, *4th All India Handloom Census 2019–20*.

The official brief reports 2,823,382 handlooms and 3,522,512 handloom workers: 2,673,891 weavers and 848,621 allied workers. It reports 25.46 lakh female workers and an average of 207 working days per year. The source also reports 31.44 lakh handloom households in the census brief.

The state totals are uneven. The source identifies Assam, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Manipur as the four states with the largest handloom-worker populations in its table.

**Implication:** The supply base is large enough to support multiple product and regional propositions, but the supply network is likely to be heterogeneous by technique, cluster, material, capacity and documentation. Any scale claim would need current cluster-level capacity and quality evidence.

**Caveat:** The census is six years old at this research cut-off and only addresses handlooms. It does not show the number of active artisans available for export-grade, repeatable, small-batch production today.

Source: <https://www.handlooms.gov.in/assets/img/Statistics/Background_note_on_handloom_sector.pdf> (census period 2019–20; accessed 26 September 2026).

### Government quality and market-positioning infrastructure

**Source:** India Handloom Brand, Ministry of Textiles, current official website.

The programme says it exists to build consumer trust in raw materials, processing, embellishment, weaving and design; ensure social and environmental compliance; create a niche market for high-quality handloom products for younger consumers and export markets; and increase weaver earnings. The site records an evaluation meeting on 27 March 2025 that granted registration to 83 products of 80 applicants.

**Implication:** India already has public quality/provenance infrastructure that can be relevant to product authentication and export storytelling where a product qualifies and the brand uses it accurately.

**Caveat:** Government registration or branding is not equivalent to luxury positioning, customer demand, or a complete chain-of-custody system for every product.

Source: <https://www.indiahandloombrand.gov.in/> (current page; accessed 26 September 2026).

### GI protection and authorised use

**Source:** Intellectual Property India, Geographical Indications Registry and the Geographical Indications of Goods Act, 1999.

The official registry lists Indian textile and handloom GIs, including Pochampally Ikat, Chanderi Fabric, Kotpad Handloom Fabric, Gamosa of Assam, Arani Silk and Barabanki Handloom. Registry records distinguish the registered indication and authorised users. The Act creates the Geographical Indications Registry and provides the legal framework for registration and authorised use.

**Implication:** GI status can be a provenance and naming variable for some craft lines, but the underlying rights and authorised-user status need to be checked product by product.

**Caveat:** A GI does not by itself certify a particular brand’s quality, exclusivity or ethical practice. Using a GI name in commerce may raise authorisation and misrepresentation questions.

Sources: <https://search.ipindia.gov.in/GIRPublicSearch/> and <https://ipindia.gov.in/page-content/geographical-indications-act-1999> (accessed 26 September 2026).

## 3. Export evidence for relevant categories and markets

### FY2024–25 handicraft exports

**Source:** Export Promotion Council for Handicrafts (EPCH), *39th Annual Report 2024–25*, reporting period ended **31 March 2025**.

EPCH reports handicraft exports, excluding hand-knotted carpets, of ₹33,122.79 crore in FY2024–25 versus ₹32,758.80 crore in FY2023–24, up 1.11% in rupee terms. In US dollars, exports declined from $3,956.46M to $3,917.89M, down 0.97%.

Relevant category movements in dollar terms were:

- handprinted textiles: +28.83%;
- embroidered and crochetted goods: +6.87%;
- shawls as artwares: +18.52%;
- zari and zari goods: +41.40%;
- woodwares: +3.84%; and
- miscellaneous handicrafts: –11.72%.

EPCH’s destination summary reports dollar exports increased to UAE (+21.72%), UK (+5.93%) and LAC (+15.66%), while USA exports declined 6.25%. The report says the data are compiled from DGCI&S sources.

**Implication:** Relevant craft-textile export categories have shown positive recent movement even while aggregate handicraft exports were broadly flat in dollars. This is evidence for category-level investigation, not proof of premium demand or attractive margins.

**Caveat:** HS-code data combine products across price points, end uses, channels and quality levels. Currency movement also affects the rupee/dollar comparison.

Source: <https://epch.in/sites/default/files/electionofcoa/EPCH_Annual_Report_2024-25.pdf> (reporting period ended 31 March 2025; accessed 26 September 2026), pp. 5–7.

### Current EPCH country/category table

**Source:** EPCH live export table, sourced to DGCI&S and marked “based on 185 HS Codes”; accessed **26 September 2026**.

The table currently reports 2025–26 values in ₹ crore for selected categories. Examples relevant to a textile/home direction:

| Category | Total | UAE | USA | UK |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Handprinted textiles | 3,952.50 | 1,690.03 | 655.67 | 86.71 |
| Embroidered & crochetted goods | 4,289.20 | 94.38 | 2,242.83 | 222.67 |
| Shawls as artwares | 4.35 | 0.02 | 0.44 | 0.05 |
| Zari & zari goods | 40.55 | 3.84 | 2.11 | 1.31 |

The same table reports 2025–26 woodwares of ₹7,160.96 crore, with USA at ₹3,056.44 crore and UK at ₹437.90 crore; this may be relevant only if the eventual range includes non-textile home/lifestyle products.

**Implication:** The mix by destination is materially different: UAE is particularly large in the reported handprinted-textile category, while the USA is much larger for embroidered/crochetted goods. This supports SKU/category-specific validation rather than assuming one global assortment.

**Caveat:** The page has no visible publication date and the exact period-status convention should be confirmed with EPCH/DGCI&S before using it for a financial model. The data are trade values, not retail sales or luxury-market sales.

Source: <https://www.epch.in/sites/default/files/policies/exportsofhandicrafts.htm> (current table; accessed 26 September 2026).

### Ministry of Textiles view of handloom export markets

**Source:** Ministry of Textiles, *Annual Report 2024–25*.

The report describes handloom exports as including fabrics, bed linen, table linen, toilet and kitchen linen, towels, curtains, cushions and pads, tapestries, upholstery, carpets and floor coverings. It identifies major handloom export centres including Karur, Panipat, Varanasi and Kannur, and names the USA, UK, Germany, Italy, France, Japan, Saudi Arabia, Australia, Netherlands and UAE among major importing countries.

**Implication:** The official sector description supports a broad home/lifestyle interpretation of Indian handloom, not only apparel or saris.

**Caveat:** The report is descriptive and does not provide a premium/luxury split, customer acquisition economics or product-level export growth for each named centre.

Source: <https://texmin.nic.in/sites/default/files/MOT%202024-25%20English%20Report%2012.03.2025.pdf> (FY2024–25 report; accessed 26 September 2026), handloom section.

## 4. Strategic buyer, platform and channel signals

### Aditya Birla Fashion and Retail

**Source:** ABFRL investor presentation for the quarter and year ended 31 March 2025, dated **23 May 2025**.

The presentation describes the company’s “most comprehensive ethnic portfolio,” including designer-led brands and premium ethnic brands. For FY25 it reports:

- TASVA revenue growth of 44% and availability across 67 stores, with 25+ stores planned for FY26;
- Jaypore revenue growth of about 14%, retail sales growth of 20%+, five stores added and 29 stores at year-end; and
- designer-led brand revenue growth of about 40%.

ABFRL’s FY2024–25 annual-report material describes Jaypore as an artisanal brand across home, apparel and accessories, and says ethnic wear is one of the largest and fastest-growing Indian apparel categories, driven by premiumisation, occasion wear and quality demand.

**Implication:** A large Indian fashion operator is publicly reporting investment in and scaling of premium ethnic and artisanal brands across store, digital and category extensions. This is relevant evidence for a future strategic-buyer universe and for understanding the scale expectations of an institutional operator.

**Caveat:** The category-growth statement is the company’s management view. The disclosures do not establish that ABFRL is seeking acquisitions, that it would buy a young craft brand, or what customer/revenue/margin thresholds would be required.

Sources: <https://www.abfrl.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Investor-PPT-Q4-FY25.pdf> (23 May 2025; accessed 26 September 2026), pp. 31–35; <https://www.cms.adityabirla.com/uploads/ABFRL_Integrated_Annual_Report_FY_24_25_3c814ee239.pdf> (FY2024–25 annual report; accessed 26 September 2026).

### Reliance retail and brands platform

**Source:** Reliance Industries Limited, *Integrated Annual Report 2024–25*, and Reliance Brands Limited financial statements for the year ended **31 March 2025**.

RIL reports FY25 retail revenue of ₹330,943 crore and describes fashion and lifestyle as one of its core consumption baskets, delivered through stores and digital platforms. It describes strategic partnerships with international brands as part of Reliance Retail’s position in fashion and lifestyle.

Reliance Brands Limited’s FY25 accounts show investments in multiple fashion/lifestyle subsidiaries and joint ventures, including Genesis Colors, Reliance Brands Luxury Fashion, Zegna South Asia, Brooks Brothers India and other brand vehicles. The accounts report total non-current investments of ₹2,563.02 crore at 31 March 2025.

**Implication:** A large domestic retail platform has the financial and distribution infrastructure to own, operate, partner with or scale brands. This is evidence of a plausible type of strategic buyer or channel partner, not evidence of a specific transaction thesis.

**Caveat:** Book value of investments, legal-entity structure and joint ventures are not comparable to an acquisition valuation. The disclosures do not reveal the purchase price, revenue multiples or acquisition criteria for each brand.

Sources: <https://www.ril.com/ar2024-25/index.html> (FY2024–25 annual report; accessed 26 September 2026); <https://www.ril.com/sites/default/files/2025-07/Reliance_Brands_Limited.pdf> (financial statements for year ended 31 March 2025; accessed 26 September 2026), pp. 31–32.

### Public evidence on a 3–4 year exit

The sources reviewed establish that large Indian retail groups are building fashion, lifestyle, luxury and artisanal portfolios. They do **not** establish:

- a normal time-to-exit for Indian craft or luxury brands;
- the valuation premium for artisan provenance, a GI, a maker network or a traceability system;
- whether strategic buyers prefer apparel, home, gifting, hospitality supply or an omnichannel platform;
- the revenue, contribution margin, repeat rate or geographic mix required for acquisition; or
- whether a 3–4 year sale is more likely than continued operation, minority investment or a licensing/partnership outcome.

These remain diligence questions rather than conclusions.

## 5. Export-market rules and operating constraints

### United States

**Textile identity and care.** The FTC says most covered textile and wool products need labels stating fibre content, country of origin and the identity of the responsible business. Its Care Labeling Rule requires care instructions for covered wearing apparel and certain piece goods, supported by a reasonable basis. The FTC’s cotton guidance explicitly includes household textile products such as draperies, floor coverings, furnishings and bedding.

Sources: <https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/threading-your-way-through-labeling-requirements-under-textile-wool-acts> (accessed 26 September 2026); <https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/care-labeling-textile-wearing-apparel-certain-piece-goods-text> (accessed 26 September 2026); <https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/calling-it-cotton-labeling-advertising-cotton-products-0> (accessed 26 September 2026).

**Classification, tariffs and low-value shipping.** USITC states that the HTS sets tariff rates and statistical categories for merchandise imported into the US and published HTS Revision 19 on 15 September 2026. CBP’s 13 February 2026 guidance says that, effective 29 August 2025, non-exempt shipments from all countries valued at or below $800 are no longer eligible for de minimis treatment and are subject to applicable duties, taxes and fees.

Sources: <https://www.usitc.gov/harmonized_tariff_information> (current HTS page; accessed 26 September 2026); <https://www.help.cbp.gov/s/article/Article-1902> (published 13 February 2026; accessed 26 September 2026).

**Implication:** A direct-to-consumer US model cannot assume that low-value parcels will enter duty-free. SKU-level HS classification, country-of-origin analysis, fibre content, importer-of-record design and landed-cost testing are material inputs to assortment and pricing.

**Caveat:** The CBP page describes a general rule with exceptions and shipment-mode details. The applicable treatment must be checked for each product and entry path.

**Environmental claims.** The FTC Green Guides caution against broad, unqualified claims such as “green” or “eco-friendly” and expect specific claims to be substantiated.

Source: <https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/topics/truth-advertising/green-guides> (FTC page; accessed 26 September 2026) and <https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/press-releases/ftc-issues-revised-green-guides/greenguides.pdf> (2012 Guides; accessed 26 September 2026).

### United Kingdom

**Textile labelling.** GOV.UK says textile labels must show fibre content, including fur and other animal parts; different-fibre components must be separately identified. The guidance applies to England, Scotland and Wales.

Source: <https://www.gov.uk/guidance/textile-labelling> (published 31 December 2020; accessed 26 September 2026).

**UK–India CETA.** The UK government states that the UK–India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement entered into force on **15 July 2026**. The goods chapter provides for tariff liberalisation and includes rules of origin and origin-declaration materials. The government also says the agreement liberalises tariffs on most Indian exports, subject to the schedules and exclusions.

Source: <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-india-ceta-chapter-2-trade-in-goods> (published 24 July 2025; updated 15 July 2026; accessed 26 September 2026).

**Implication:** The UK market has a new India-specific trade framework, but the commercial benefit depends on the product’s commodity code, originating status, documentation and the relevant schedule. It should not be modelled as a blanket zero-duty outcome.

**Environmental claims.** The UK Competition and Markets Authority’s Green Claims Code collection was last updated on 22 January 2026. Its fashion guidance warns that claims about a product’s environmental benefit can mislead if they omit significant impacts or focus on a minor attribute.

Sources: <https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/how-to-make-environmental-claims-about-products-and-services> (updated 22 January 2026; accessed 26 September 2026); <https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/complying-with-consumer-law-in-the-fashion-retail-sector/complying-with-consumer-law-in-the-fashion-retail-sector> (accessed 26 September 2026).

### United Arab Emirates / GCC

Dubai Customs describes an integrated HS-based tariff system using the GCC’s 12-digit structure. The UAE’s Federal Authority for Identity, Citizenship, Customs & Port Security describes the GCC common customs tariff as 5% on foreign goods imported from outside the union, with exemptions and protected goods as exceptions. The UAE Federal Tax Authority says VAT is generally 5% on goods and services unless exempt or zero-rated.

Sources: <https://www.dubaicustoms.gov.ae/en/CustomsInformation/Pages/IntegratedCustomsTariff.aspx> (accessed 26 September 2026); <https://icp.gov.ae/en/customs-union-for-gcc/> (accessed 26 September 2026); <https://tax.gov.ae/en/faq.aspx?keyword=Does+VAT+apply+to+all+goods+and+services> (page last updated 22 May 2024; accessed 26 September 2026).

**Implication:** UAE pricing and fulfilment require HS classification, importer/distributor structure, customs valuation, VAT treatment and any product-specific restriction checks. UAE can also function as a regional logistics or retail hub, but the sources here do not measure re-export demand for Indian textiles.

**Caveat:** “Generally 5%” is not a product-specific landed-cost conclusion. Dubai Customs’ HS search and a local customs adviser should be used for the actual SKU.

### European Union as a likely “etc.” market

The European Commission’s 2025–30 ESPR working plan identifies textiles, with a focus on apparel, as a priority product group. The Commission says textile-specific Digital Product Passport requirements will be defined through a future delegated act and that the DPP is intended to improve transparency and traceability across textile value chains.

Sources: <https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/news/2025-2030-working-plan-2025-07-11_en> (11 July 2025; accessed 26 September 2026); <https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/digital-product-passport/textile-apparel_en> (accessed 26 September 2026); <https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1781/eng> (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781; accessed 26 September 2026).

**Implication:** Even where a future DPP is not yet mandatory for a specific product, structured product-level provenance and material data may reduce future transition costs and improve retailer diligence.

**Caveat:** The public Commission page does not yet specify all textile product requirements, dates or thresholds. It should be treated as a direction of travel, not as a current compliance checklist.

## 6. Evidence gaps to resolve before making a product or market decision

These are unresolved questions exposed by the research—not recommendations:

1. **Customer economics:** What percentage of the intended Indian top-wealth cohort buys textile/home objects, at what annual spend, and through which relationship or retail channels?
2. **Price architecture:** Which products can sustain luxury gross margins after artisan wages, sampling, rejection, packaging, shipping, duties, returns, wholesale margin and customer acquisition costs?
3. **Category choice:** Do home, apparel, occasion, gifting, collectible textiles or hospitality/interiors produce the best combination of repeat purchase, supply repeatability and export compliance?
4. **Supply capacity:** For each technique, what are current active artisans, realistic monthly capacity, lead time, defect/rework rate, material constraints and succession risk?
5. **Authenticity:** Which products can support verifiable maker, material, place and process claims, and which claims require India Handloom Brand, GI, laboratory or other third-party evidence?
6. **Export economics:** For representative SKUs, what are the US HTS code and duty, UK CETA classification/origin treatment, UAE HS code/customs/VAT treatment, delivery time and return cost?
7. **Channel proof:** What sell-through, repeat, full-price share, conversion, customer acquisition cost and returns data would a strategic buyer consider credible?
8. **Exit evidence:** Which Indian or global buyers have actually acquired comparable brands, what assets did they buy, and what operational dependencies survived the transaction?
9. **Claims risk:** Which “sustainable,” “natural,” “handmade,” “heritage,” “fair,” “organic,” “zero-waste” or “carbon” claims can be substantiated consistently across the supply network?
10. **Data recency:** How have the 2019–20 handloom workforce and cluster capacities changed by 2026, especially in remote or endangered practices?
