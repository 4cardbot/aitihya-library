# Aitihya / Pramāṇa: strategic recommendation

**Research cut-off:** 26 September 2026  
**Inputs:** `business_plans/1.md` through `business_plans/7.md`, current first-party market and regulatory sources.

## Recommendation

Build **Aitihya** as a **designer-led Indian textile interiors house for exceptional homes**. Use **Pramāṇa** as its provenance, evidence and digital product-certification registry.

The company should translate rare Indian textile knowledge into a tightly edited system of contemporary wall textiles, throws/coverlets and cushions or small table textiles. Sell first through interior designers and architects, with private-client appointments as the prestige layer. Use numbered textile editions as the halo, but make repeatable core products the revenue engine.

This is not a broad handicrafts marketplace, an apparel label, a hotel-linen supplier or an art gallery disguised as a company.

## What the seven plans agree on

1. Apparel and bridal are the wrong first category for a 3–4 year build-and-sell objective.
2. Home and architectural textiles reduce sizing friction and can be sold through professional specifiers.
3. An edited visual language is more valuable than a catalogue of every craft Anjan knows.
4. Endangered or rare techniques should create authority and scarcity, not carry all commercial volume.
5. Anjan’s field relationships become defensible only when converted into documented designs, production standards, contracts, provenance and a team.
6. A private, relationship-led launch is more appropriate than a high-street store or mass digital advertising.

## Where the plans overreach

### 1. The exit valuation is not a strategy

The proposed $50–100M exit, 3–5x revenue multiples and 12–22x EBITDA multiples are aspirations, not evidence. A buyer will pay for repeatable demand, clean accounts, transferable design rights, reliable fulfilment and a brand that works without its founder. The acquisition should be an option, not the operating assumption.

### 2. 75–88% gross margins should not be underwritten

At launch, model artisan payment, sampling, wastage, rework, finishing, packaging, quality control, trade discounts, commissions, freight and claims honestly. A 55–65% gross-margin range is a more useful initial planning hypothesis; improve it through repeatability and mix rather than by excluding real costs. Treat 65–72% as a scale target to be earned.

### 3. “Zero returns” and “zero CAC” are false comforts

Home products still have damage, colour, measurement, care and installation claims. Trade sales still have sample, travel, representation and relationship costs. Track return/claim rates and fully loaded acquisition cost by channel.

### 4. The hybrid wearable line dilutes the launch

Wraps and relaxed garments can be a later capsule, but adding apparel, bedding, upholstery, rugs, acoustic products and hospitality at launch creates too many materials, specifications and operational failure points. The first year needs one recognisable product world.

### 5. “Anthropological luxury” is not yet a customer proposition

Anthropology is Anjan’s advantage, not necessarily the name of the category. The customer should understand the object, its visual language, its material excellence and why it belongs in a contemporary room within seconds. The deeper research becomes the provenance layer.

## The launch architecture

Use a 70/20/10 planning split:

| Layer | Product | Role |
|---|---|---|
| 70% core | 6–8 repeatable cushions, throws or coverlets | Repeat orders, room completion, designer specifications |
| 20% editions | 3–4 numbered wall textiles or larger textile objects | Scarcity, press, collector interest, higher AOV |
| 10% bespoke | A small number of custom panels or room commissions | Authority and learning, not the operating model |

Launch with approximately **12 designs**, but fewer materials and production partners. Use two production-ready craft families for the core and one rarer technique for the halo. Do not select them only because they are endangered. Score every candidate for:

- visual distinctiveness in a contemporary room;
- material stability and care requirements;
- artisan capacity and realistic lead time;
- repeatability without flattening the technique;
- quality-control measurability;
- export and installation suitability;
- community consent, attribution and benefit-sharing;
- target-price feasibility after trade margin and fulfilment costs.

Indicative domestic test bands, not final prices:

- cushions or paired small textiles: ₹15,000–₹40,000;
- throws/coverlets: ₹50,000–₹1.5 lakh;
- textile wall panels: ₹2–₹6 lakh;
- bespoke commissions: ₹8 lakh and upward, quoted only after a repeatable process exists.

These must be tested against real deposits and full landed costs. The price premium over established Indian lifestyle products must be visible in design, material, finishing, scale, scarcity and service—not only in the story.

## The actual customer

Replace “India’s top 1%” with three buying roles:

1. **Specifier:** the interior designer or architect who chooses and orders the product.
2. **Approver:** the wealthy homeowner, family office or collector who accepts the room and price.
3. **Amplifier:** the designer, gallery, hotel or cultural institution that gives the work credibility and introductions.

The first commercial wedge is the specifier. A repeat relationship with 20–30 good design studios is more useful than a large list of wealthy names.

## Channel and geography sequence

1. **India first:** Mumbai and Delhi NCR, using the strongest warm introductions. Add Bengaluru only when a specific account justifies it.
2. **UAE second:** run a Dubai/Abu Dhabi trade and private-client pilot. EPCH data show the UAE is a major destination for Indian handprinted textiles, but that is a trade signal, not proof of luxury demand.
3. **One western-market pilot:** choose London or a US design representative based on actual partner access. Do not launch the US, UK and UAE simultaneously. US SKU-level labelling, origin, care and landed-cost compliance must be built into the product file before shipment.

Use a portable sample library, trade price list, accurate lead times, room photography, material swatches, installation guidance and a responsive account owner. Avoid permanent flagships until a repeatable local sales pattern pays for one.

## Operating moat

Create a company-owned operating library containing:

- approved master samples and tolerances;
- yarn, dye, weave, colour and finishing specifications;
- lead times, capacity and cost by workshop;
- quality checks, repair and rejection procedures;
- product care, labelling and export records;
- maker attribution and provenance permissions;
- design-development records and rights;
- customer, project and reorder history.

Use preferred multi-year partnerships and capacity reservations rather than claiming exclusive control of whole communities. Traditional cultural expressions should not be treated as company-owned IP by default. New co-developed compositions, product designs, images and brand assets need clear agreements; attribution, consent and benefit-sharing should be explicit. WIPO’s guidance supports respectful engagement with communities and warns against using traditional cultural expressions without consent or shared benefits.

## 90-day validation gate

### Days 1–30

- Interview 15 Indian luxury residential designers, 5 UAE/UK/US designers and 10 private buyers.
- Ask for recent purchase examples, budget, lead-time tolerance, supplier failures and approval process.
- Choose two core craft families and one halo technique using the scorecard.

### Days 31–60

- Make 8–12 prototypes, not a full collection.
- Cost each product after artisan pay, finishing, QC, packaging, selling cost and expected claims.
- Prepare trade samples, specifications, care instructions, provenance records and realistic delivery dates.

### Days 61–90

- Hold private presentations in India and one export market.
- Seek at least five paid pilot projects, three unrelated buying organisations and two export trials.
- Kill products that receive admiration but no deposit at a sustainable price.

The first hire should be a commercially experienced textile/interiors product lead or operations lead, depending on which capability Anjan does not already have. The founder should remain central to curation and relationships while daily fulfilment becomes transferable.

## Exit-readiness milestones

By the end of year three, the business should aim to demonstrate—not merely forecast—:

- a recognisable company-owned design language;
- most revenue from carry-forward products and repeatable editions;
- repeat orders from professional design accounts;
- positive contribution by product, channel and destination;
- clean contracts, rights, provenance and export documentation;
- no single customer or artisan cluster carrying the company;
- a product and operations team that can fulfil orders without Anjan in every conversation;
- at least twelve months of clean management accounts and positive EBITDA before a sale process.

This supports a focused high-end interiors thesis, not an assumption that every luxury product will sell.
